# The Fire Pot: reimagining hotpot

*A dining table that weighs your food, prices it as you pick, and sets itself on fire. Responsibly.*

# Eight bins, a pair of tongs, and absolutely no idea

There's a hot pot stall in a Singapore hawker centre that I ate at all the way through my master's at NTU. The arrangement is beautifully simple. Take a bowl, take a pair of tongs, walk along the stand and load up whatever you fancy. Hand it over at the end. They weigh it, ask which broth and how spicy, and cook it. Your number gets called. It's fast, it's cheap, and you get exactly the bowl you asked for.

I ate there constantly, and I was guessing every single time.

Some bins I could read on sight. Others held a pale, smooth sphere that could have been fish, could have been pork, could have been a very confident potato. So I picked by shape. I learned what any of it tasted like only once it came back cooked, in a broth I'd chosen off a sign written in a language I can't read, at a price I found out at the till.

Everyone else seemed to know. The staff knew. The regulars knew. The answers existed. They were just somewhere other than where I was standing, which was at the stand, holding tongs, holding up the queue.

**The Fire Pot** puts the answers exactly where I was standing.

# The table is the screen

The tabletop is a sheet of projector screen fabric stretched over plywood, with eight bins set into it. A projector hangs above, mounted as close to the ceiling as it will go so its throw covers the whole table, and a camera sits up there with it. Everything you see, from the ingredient names and prices to your running cart and the whole checkout, is light landing on that surface, and the camera watches your hands so the table knows what you're reaching for.

That's the design brief in one line: a person at a hot pot stand has a bowl in one hand and tongs in the other, so everything has to happen where their hands already are.

# It waits like something alive

Every bin wears a soft golden ring that breathes, brightening and fading slowly, each one a little out of step with its neighbours, so the surface is never entirely still.

What gives away an empty table is the fireball. Left to itself, a small flame wanders the surface, drifting from bin to bin at browsing pace, as though someone invisible were working along the row and taking their time about it. Underneath, the low crackle of a fire.

Walk up, put a hand over the table, and it comes to attention. All eight bins label themselves at once: the name of what's in them, and the price per 100 g underneath.

Instant Noodles, $1.50. Lotus Root Slices, $1.40. Dried Eel Strips, $5.00. You know what everything is and exactly what it costs before you pick up a single thing.

# Reach for a bin and it catches fire

Put a hand over the table and the wandering flame becomes yours. The fireball leaves off drifting and tracks your palm instead, staying with you wherever you move.

Carry it over a bin and the fire changes hands. The ball goes out, the bin's golden ring gives way to a burning one, and it is the bin that's alight now, in a colour of its own. Lift your hand away from the bin and the flame comes back to you, the bin settling behind you into its slow gold breathing again.

While a bin is burning, the middle of the table fills with the thing you're pointing at:

- **What it is**: the full name, in large type
- **Veg, non-veg, or egg**: a coloured dot with the word beside it
- **Calories per 100 g**: Button Mushrooms come in at 22, Instant Noodles at 450, which is the single most useful number nobody ever tells you
- **What it actually tastes like**: one honest sentence, written for someone who has never eaten it

That last one is the whole reason this project exists. *Lotus Root Slices: stays crunchy even after cooking. Mild, faintly sweet, never goes soft.* *Dried Shrimp: small but intense. Deeply savoury, so a little goes a long way.* *Fish Balls: bouncy and mild. A safe pick if you would rather not chase strong flavours.*

Move your hand along the row and you can read your way through all eight before you commit to one.

# The bowl fills and the bill fills with it

Scoop something into your bowl and it appears in the cart along the near edge of the table, with its weight and its price. Scoop more and the number climbs. Every item you've picked stays listed, and the running total sits underneath the lot, ticking upward as it goes.

This is the number I never had in Singapore. It means you can take 60 g of the $5.00 eel because you can see it's costing you $3.00, and you can be generous with the $1.00 potato because you can see that it barely registers. The expensive decisions and the cheap ones stop feeling the same.

Take too much? Tip some back into the bin. The weight drops, the price drops with it, and the total settles to the new figure. The table simply reports what's in your bowl at this moment, so you can rearrange your order as many times as you like right up until you're happy with it.

# The same table, in Chinese

One button, marked `EN | 中文`, switches the entire table over. Every ingredient name, every tasting note, every price, the broth descriptions, the spice levels, the checkout. All of it.

鱼丸. 藕片. 牛肉丸.

It switches back just as fast, so two people reading different languages can share the same table and take turns.

# Choosing a broth you can genuinely choose

When you're done picking, the table turns into the ordering screen, and the first question is the broth. Three of them, each presented with the two facts that actually decide it:

- **Classic Mala Broth.** *Very spicy.* Numbing and fiery, built on Sichuan pepper and chilli. The boldest broth here.
- **Mushroom Vegan Broth.** *Not spicy.* Deep and savoury with no meat at all. Earthy rather than rich.
- **Collagen Bone Broth.** *Not spicy.* Milky and slow simmered. Rich on the tongue but completely mild.

Each one says whether it's spicy and whether it's vegetarian right on the card, in your language, before you commit. This is the sign I stood in front of in Singapore and couldn't read, rewritten so that a first-timer can pick with confidence.

# Then how hot you want it

Spice arrives as the same three cards, stacked by heat. Hot sits at the top of the table, furthest from you; mild at the bottom, nearest to hand. Each card counts its own heat in chillies: one, two, three.

- **Hot.** Serious heat. Worth choosing only if you already know you like it.
- **Medium.** Properly spicy and still comfortable. The most ordered level.
- **Mild.** A gentle warmth. You taste the broth first and the chilli after.

Pick one and the table moves on to the total. A row of chillies means the same thing in every language, which is rather the point.

# Pay from where you're standing

The table adds up your ingredients, your broth and your spice, shows you the total, and projects a QR code onto the table in front of you.

Scan it with your phone and you get an itemised receipt: every ingredient with its weight and its price, the broth you picked, the spice level you picked, and the total at the bottom. Pay on the phone, and the table notices immediately.

It clears down to a single large token number, with two lines under it: **hand your bowl to the staff**, and **we'll call this number when it's ready.** The staff take the bowl away and cook it. Your number gets called. Exactly the same ending as the stall in Singapore. The difference is everything that happened before it.

# The other half of the table: the staff dashboard

Everything above is what a diner sees. The staff run the table from a browser on any device on the same network, and it's where the fiddly parts of a real restaurant shift live.

One switch at the top says **Serving**. On, and the table is open for business. Off, and it stops billing so staff can reach across the bins, swap trays and work on the table without anything registering as a customer's pick.

**Bins.** Eight cards, one per bin, each showing its live weight as it changes. A dropdown on every card says what that bin currently holds, so refilling bin 3 with something different is a two-second job. Let a bin fall below 50 g and its card raises a restock alert, which saves anyone the walk to go and look. The scales themselves live here too: an empty bin can be zeroed, and any bin can be calibrated against a known weight. Zeroing runs on all eight at once, since "the bins are empty" tends to be true of the lot of them at the same moment.

**Setup.** This is the tab that teaches the table where it is. The staff mark the four corners of the physical table on a live camera view, then lay a grid over it to say where each bin sits, and finally nudge a second grid with the arrow keys, watching the real projected light on the real table, until it lines up with the actual bins. It's the one job that has to be done by a human looking at the thing itself, and it's built to be done that way.

**Capture.** The table can photograph its own bins to build a training dataset, one burst at a time, at whatever interval suits the person rearranging the tray between shots. Those images export straight out to Edge Impulse or Roboflow.

**Developer.** The engineering window. A live hand skeleton overlaid on the camera feed showing exactly what the tracker sees, the classifier's guesses as they happen, camera controls for exposure and white balance, a health readout for every process, and a live plot of each load cell, raw against filtered, with the filter's own settings adjustable while you watch the line change shape.

# What it's made of

The tabletop rests on eight load cells, one under each bin, read by a **Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32S3** that streams all eight channels up to the host over USB. The host is an **ASUS NUC 14**, running the brains in Python and the projected table itself in openFrameworks. Above it all, an ordinary projector mounted almost against the ceiling, high enough that its throw covers the full table, with a webcam beside it and Google's MediaPipe doing the hand tracking.

Everything past this point is the engineering. If you want to build one, the next section is the parts and the wiring. If you want to know how it works, the section after that is the architecture, the calibration, and the fire.

# Wire the load cells

[IMAGE: docs/img/firepot-loadcells.png]
*Eight identical channels. One shared clock, eight separate data lines, one USB cable out. The KiCad source is in the Schematics section below, and at `hardware/firepot-loadcells/` in the repo.*

Every connection is a screw terminal or a lever connector, so the whole harness comes apart again.

- Solder the female header strip to the 5 x 7 cm prototype board and plug the XIAO into it
- Put four screw terminal blocks around the XIAO: one power and clock block and one data block per island
- Give every HX711 its own screw terminal block for the run back to the XIAO
- Split 3V3, ground and the clock with the PCT-215 lever connectors, one feed in and four out, three per island

**Short VCC and VDD together on each HX711.** Both go to 3V3.

Each load cell has four wires into its own HX711: red to `E+`, black to `E-`, green to `A+`, white to `A-`. Check the colours against your own cell.

Each HX711 then takes 3V3 and ground from the rails, shares the clock on `D0` with all seven others, and sends its own data line back:

```
D3  / GPIO4     bin 0    far left
D4  / GPIO5     bin 1    far centre-left
D10 / GPIO9     bin 2    far centre-right
D9  / GPIO8     bin 3    far right
D5  / GPIO6     bin 4    near left
D6  / GPIO43    bin 5    near centre-left
D8  / GPIO7     bin 6    near centre-right
D7  / GPIO44    bin 7    near right
```

The eight bins sit as two islands of four, each island holding one pair from each row. `D3` to `D6` run to the left island and `D7` to `D10` to the right, and the right island counts backwards because `D10` down to `D7` sit top to bottom on that header. Wire it in that order and the harness stays in physical order.

Leave `D1` and `D2` unused. `D2` is `GPIO3`, a strapping pin sampled at reset.

Mount each cell whichever way round suits the frame. Calibration works out the sign per bin.

# Get the code

The projected table and the Python processes behind it live in one repository, and it belongs inside an openFrameworks tree: the Visual Studio project walks five levels up to find openFrameworks, so `apps/myApps/hotpot-table` is where it expects to be.

Download [openFrameworks 0.12.1](https://openframeworks.cc/download/) for Visual Studio, unzip it, and clone into it:

```
cd openFrameworks/apps/myApps
git clone https://github.com/rahuljeyaraj/hotpot-table
```

Everything from here on runs from that folder.

# Flash the XIAO

Flash the firmware with [PlatformIO](https://platformio.org/):

```
cd openFrameworks/apps/myApps/hotpot-table/firmware/loadcells
pio run -t upload
pio device monitor
```

A working board prints this and then keeps going:

```
# hx711 loadcell aggregator, 8 cells, raw counts
# built Aug 28 2026 11:04:12
# probe: all 8 cells reporting
# ready
raw 83422 -211904 84001 82755 -210338 83190 84772 83006
```

If a cell is missing, the probe names the pin and says which of two faults it is:

```
# probe: no data line activity from bins 2 5 (D10/GPIO9 D6/GPIO43)
#   bin 2 D10/GPIO9 floating
#   bin 5 D6/GPIO43 driven-high
```

- `floating`: an open data wire, or an HX711 with no power. Check that terminal block.
- `driven-high`: the board has power and is holding the line up. Check the clock reaching it, and the board itself.

# Install the software

**The table.** Four addons are needed. `ofxGui`, `ofxOsc` and `ofxNetwork` come with openFrameworks. [ofxFlowTools](https://github.com/moostrik/ofxFlowTools), the fluid solver behind the fire, is a separate download that goes in `openFrameworks/addons/`.

It needs a patch before it will build, kept in the repo at `of/patches/ofxFlowTools.patch`:

```
cd openFrameworks/addons/ofxFlowTools
git apply ../../apps/myApps/hotpot-table/of/patches/ofxFlowTools.patch
```

That does two things. It replaces two constructs the current compiler rejects, `std::bind2nd` and an unqualified `min`. And it fixes the density lookup in the buoyancy and diffusion shaders, which sample a texture that is at a different resolution from the one they render at, so the fire is pulled around by density that sits at double the coordinates of the real thing. There is more on that one in the next section.

Open `of/hotpot-table/hotpot-table.sln` in Visual Studio and build the **Debug** configuration, which is the binary the launcher starts. Set `HOTPOT_OF_BIN` if you would rather it ran something else.

**Python.** One interpreter for the whole tree, installed from the repository root:

```
pip install -r python/requirements.txt
```

**Three values for your own rig.**

- `SCALE_PORT` in `python/hotpot/core/main.py` is the XIAO's serial port, `COM5` here. `pio device monitor` names yours.
- `camera.windows_device_index` in `config/system.json` picks the webcam, `0` by default. Raise it if the host has another camera it prefers.
- `of/hotpot-table/bin/data/display.txt` picks the monitor, as the projector's desktop origin in `x,y` form. Start the app once and it writes the file and logs every monitor it found with its origin. Put the projector's in. This rig's reads `-1920,144`.

# Start it

```
python run.py
```

That starts and supervises everything, waits for each process to report ready before starting the next, and writes one merged log to the terminal and to `logs/hotpot-<date>.log`.

- `python run.py --replace` stops whatever is running and starts fresh
- `python run.py --stop` stops a detached instance
- `python run.py --only core,of` starts the table alone, with the camera unplugged
- `python run.py --no-restart` leaves a crashed process dead so you can read its traceback

The dashboard is at `http://<the-host>:8090` from any device on the same network, a phone included.

# Calibrate the geometry

Three steps in the dashboard's **Setup** tab, in the order the cards appear. Turn the **Serving** switch off first: every one of them is refused while it is on.

- **Mark the four corners.** Tap `Set table corners`. A quad appears on the live camera feed with a handle at each corner. Drag each handle onto the matching real corner of the table. The handles have fixed roles, near-left, near-right, far-right, far-left, so read the real table rather than the screen. Holding a handle brings up a 4x magnifier. Tap `Confirm`, and both the Setup and Live tabs switch to a flattened, top-down view of the table.
- **Lay the bin grid on it.** Four horizontal lines and eight vertical ones, dragged onto the flattened view: a horizontal line sets a whole row's edge, a vertical line sets a whole column's edge. `Load measured layout` starts you from the CAD positions, `Undo` steps back, and `Save` commits it.
- **Nudge the projector grid.** Click one of the twelve lines in the list, or Tab to it, and press the arrow keys. Up and down move a horizontal line, left and right move a vertical one, one pixel per press and ten with Shift held. Each press reaches the projector immediately and there is nothing to save. Work through the lines until each projected golden halo sits on its real bin, watching the table rather than the screen.

# Calibrate the scales

**Serving** stays off for this too.

Go to the **Bins** tab, empty all eight bins, and tap `Tare all 8 bins`. It zeroes every bin from one 2-second reading.

Then, per bin, tap `Calibrate`, put a known weight in that bin, type its mass and confirm. A result under ten counts per gram is refused, which means the cell has come loose or the weight was too light.

Finally, set what each bin holds from the dropdown on its card.

# Check it

Turn **Serving** on and stand at the table.

- Eight golden halos, breathing, and with nobody at the table a flame wandering from bin to bin
- A hand over the table brings up all eight names and prices
- A hand over one bin moves the flame onto it and fills the middle column with that ingredient's details
- Something taken from a bin appears in the cart with a weight that matches your kitchen scales
- Putting it back takes it off again

To check the table before the load cells are wired, start `python run.py --only core,of` and open the **Developer** tab. It has a `Pick` and a `Put back` button per bin, and they drive the whole flow through to the token number.

# Under the plywood

Everything so far happens on the top surface. Underneath it there are five processes, four transports, about thirty-five thousand lines of code with another twenty thousand of tests behind it, and a fluid simulation borrowed from an addon that turned out to have been running the wrong half of itself the whole time.

This section is the engineering. It assumes you have read the build guide, and it goes in the order the system actually goes: the shape first, then the places where the physical world refused to behave the way the design document said it would.

# Five processes, and why the split falls where it does

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-processes.svg]
*Five processes, four transports. The arrows are the only way any of these five learn anything about each other.*

`python run.py` is the whole start command. It launches every process, waits for each one to print `HOTPOT-READY <name>` on its own stdout before it starts the next tier, merges all of their output into one colour-prefixed stream that also lands in `logs/hotpot-<date>.log`, restarts anything that dies on a ladder that begins at one second and doubles to ten, and marks a process failed after five deaths inside a rolling sixty-second window rather than spinning on a build that cannot start. Every child is launched in its own process group, so killing the group kills its grandchildren too. An orphaned process holding the camera or squatting on port 8765 is the single most annoying failure mode a system like this has, and it is the one thing the launcher exists to make impossible.

The tiers are an ordering, not a dependency. Every client reconnects to core forever, with the same backoff, so any process can start, die and restart in any order and the system still converges. Waiting for readiness buys tidy logs. It does not buy correctness, because correctness was never allowed to depend on it.

**`camera` owns the device and nothing else does.** It opens the webcam, negotiates a mode, applies whatever exposure and white balance were locked to `state/camera_settings.json`, and writes every frame into shared memory. It also serves an MJPEG stream on port 8081, which is what the dashboard's live view is looking at.

**`tracker` runs MediaPipe Hands** on frames it reads out of that shared memory, applies the camera to stage homography itself, and fires one UDP datagram per tracked frame at both `of` and `core`.

**`classifier` crops each bin out of a warped frame** and says what is in it. It is also the process that photographs bins to build a training set.

**`core` is everything a diner is actually being charged for**: the state machine, the cart, the price list, the bin map, the geometry, the load cells over USB serial, the order database, the QR code, and the staff dashboard served over HTTP and WebSocket on port 8090. It is the TCP server every other process dials into.

**`of` draws.** It receives a complete description of the table sixty times a second and paints exactly that. Every word on the surface, including the units, arrives already translated. The `sub` field is the finished string `"$1.40/100g"`, symbol and suffix and all, rather than a number and a currency code for C++ to assemble. Even the `g` is a locale string, because Chinese wants `/100克`, and an f-string with `g` baked into it would have left the price line as the one part of every plate that stayed in English after the switch. Every dwell fraction is a 0 to 1 value core computed, so the ring filling on a button and the timer that actually fires are one clock. Adding Chinese to the table meant two data files, `data/locales/zh.json` and the Chinese fields in `data/catalogue.json`, and zero lines of C++.

That last split is the one that pays. It means the table's appearance can be rewritten without touching anything that bills, and the billing can be rewritten without opening a shader.

# One line of JSON, sixty times a second

The control link is newline-delimited JSON over TCP, and it carries the whole table:

```
{"t":"state","seq":48213,"ts":1756377611.42,"mode":"serving","phase":"selecting",
 "locale":"en","idle_attract":false,"fire_active":true,
 "bins":[{"i":0,"label":"Lotus Root Slices","sub":"$1.40/100g",
          "rect":[115.9,209.1,252.0,301.2],
          "grams":312,"picked":64,"price":0.9,
          "hl":"hover","stock":"ok","resolved":true,
          "info":{"diet":"veg","meta":"74 kcal / 100g",
                  "desc":"Stays crunchy even after cooking. Mild, faintly sweet."}}, ...],
 "total":{"amount":4.6,"text":"$4.60","label":"Total"},
 "widgets":[{"id":"done","kind":"button","label":"Next","x":1078,"y":937,
             "w":154.7,"h":76,"dwell":0.42,"hover":true,"selected":false,
             "enabled":true,"style":"primary"}],
 "screen":{"title":""},"overlay":{"kind":"none"}}
```

**Sixty times a second whether or not anything changed.** A fixed-rate stream is what lets the renderer treat silence as a fault instead of as news. `of` measures seconds since the last state message and puts a NO SIGNAL marker in the corner past a threshold, which it could not do if a quiet table also meant a quiet socket.

**There is exactly one rule about dropping, and it is applied on both sides.** A line is either written to a live socket or it is counted as dropped and the send returns false. It is never buffered forever. Sending while the link is down drops at once, because there is nowhere to put it. Sending while the link is up but the outbound queue has filled means the peer has stopped draining, so the link is closed and the peer reconnects. The queue is sized at 256 lines, which at sixty a second is 4.3 seconds of a peer ignoring us, and it is deliberately sized to trip before the ten-second socket timeout does. A reset link is loud and recoverable. A silently swallowed "turn serving off" wedges the table with a diner standing at it.

**One discovery worth passing on.** openFrameworks ships `ofxTCPClient`, and its `send()` appends `[/TCP]` and a literal NUL byte to every message, unconditionally, whatever you set the message delimiter to. That NUL lands inside the next line as far as Python is concerned and fails the parse on it. So the C++ side of this link never calls `send()` or `receive()` at all. It uses `sendRaw()` and `receiveRawBytes()` and does its own newline splitting, which is the same job the Python side's line reader is doing thirty lines away in another language.

The link runs on its own thread on both sides, for a reason specific to this library: `ofxTCPManager::Connect()` blocks even in non-blocking mode, because the non-blocking flag is only set after the connect returns. A core that is slow to start would otherwise freeze the table before a single frame was drawn.

# Cursors go over UDP, and the old ones are thrown away

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-cursor-drain.svg]
*The same six datagrams, and the two things a transport can do with them.*

A lost cursor packet is worthless sixteen milliseconds later. A queued one is worse than worthless: it is a hand that visibly walks back through its own history the moment the render thread catches up. So cursors get their own transport, on their own port, with one rule at the receiving end. Read the socket until it is empty, keep the highest sequence number, discard everything else.

There are actually two rules, and they are different, which is the part that took a bug to learn. Within one drain, keep the highest sequence. Across drains, refuse any sequence at or below one already accepted. UDP reorders, so a datagram that lost its race arrives on the next frame, after its own successor has already been drawn. Accepting it steps the cursor backwards for exactly one frame, which is a visible twitch and the miniature version of the problem the first rule exists to prevent.

An empty hands array in an accepted datagram is a real statement and does clear the cursor. A datagram that simply failed to arrive leaves the last one standing. Those two look identical if you are careless and are opposite in meaning: one is an empty table, the other is a dropped packet.

**And a Windows-specific trap, found the hard way.** If any earlier send to a local UDP port ever drew back an ICMP port-unreachable, Windows remembers, and every subsequent receive on that socket fails until it is explicitly told to stop caring. It broke every receiver on the rig and looked exactly like a tracker that had stopped sending.

# Frames live in shared memory

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-frame-ring.svg]
*Eight slots, about 47 MB, and a publication rule that is three writes in a fixed order.*

Six megabytes of pixels thirty times a second belongs in a page table rather than in a socket. The camera writes into a ring of eight slots in shared memory and the tracker and the classifier read out of it. Nothing takes a lock; the ordering of the writes is what makes it safe.

Publication is ordering. The writer puts the pixels in the slot, then the slot header, then increments a global write counter last. That final increment is what a reader treats as "this frame exists", so it has to happen after everything it publishes rather than before. A reader takes the slot's frame id before copying the pixels out and again afterwards, and retries if the two disagree, which means the writer lapped it mid-copy.

At eight slots and thirty frames a second a reader has about 260 milliseconds before it can be lapped, so a torn read should be unobservable in practice. The retry is still there, and the tests force a torn read by hand rather than trusting that it never happens, on the principle that a check which cannot fail proves nothing.

`core` never imports this module, and that omission is the enforcement. The process that owns the money works in grams and rectangles and has no way to touch a pixel, because the code to do it was never linked into it.

A camera that dies and comes back creates a **new** segment, so every reader opens the ring lazily and reopens it after any failure. A reader that attached once at startup would be holding a corpse forever after the first camera restart, and would report it as "no frames" rather than as "the thing I am attached to no longer exists".

# Four coordinate spaces, and the one that is canonical

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-coordinate-spaces.svg]
*Millimetres, stage pixels, camera pixels, and the fluid's own pair.*

The table is 1524 by 914.4 millimetres. The projector is 1920 by 1080 pixels. Those two aspect ratios are 1.667 and 1.778, so the conversion uses **two scales, one per axis**: 1.25984 pixels per millimetre across, 1.18110 down. A single uniform scale puts the near row of bins fifty millimetres out, which on plywood is a visible finger's width, and it does it in a way that looks like a mounting error rather than an arithmetic one.

The bin layout lives as two chains of measurements that each have to sum to a table dimension, and the header enforces that at compile time:

```
across:  92 + 200 + 50 + 200 + 440 + 200 + 50 + 200 + 92 = 1524 mm
front to back:      177 + 255 + 50 + 255 + 177.4         = 914.4 mm
```

Edit a bin's width without moving the bins after it and the build fails. The same chain exists a second time in Python, because C++ cannot import a module and Python cannot include a header, and a test mirrors the header's assertions so that an edit made to one side and forgotten on the other fails a test rather than moving four trays by fifty millimetres on the rig.

**Stage pixels are canonical, and only one process holds the matrix.** The tracker applies the camera to stage homography itself and puts stage pixels on the wire. Core hit-tests stage pixels against stage rectangles. openFrameworks draws stage pixels. The three cannot disagree about where a hand is, because two of them have no way to form an opinion.

**The trap underneath all of it.** A homography solved from four points always fits those four points exactly. So it comes back reporting an average error of 0.0 pixels from 4 points, and that number is measuring nothing at all. This rig has produced a homography with a perfect score that pointed nowhere near the real table, from an operator clicking the corners in an order that paired each one with its opposite. The camera is mounted at 180 degrees, so on screen that ordering looks entirely reasonable.

Two things came out of that. The corner roles are pinned to the drag order and are never inferred from where a handle lands on screen, because an operator standing at the table knows which corner is physically nearest their left hand regardless of what the feed looks like. And the only check that can fail here is a person looking at the light on the actual table, so that is the only check the geometry store offers.

# Two bin grids, and neither one is computed from the other

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-two-grids.svg]
*Four lines across, eight down, and two separate files holding the same shape.*

A bin boundary used to be eight rectangles, dragged one at a time. That broke twice over.

It broke because nothing kept two bins in the same row level with each other. Bin 0's top edge and bin 1's top edge are meant to be the same edge, and eight independent rectangles let them differ by a few pixels: invisible in a list of numbers, very visible as one tray's halo sitting higher than its neighbour's on a real table.

And it broke because one rectangle list was being asked to serve two different physical devices. The camera's view of the table and the projector's throw onto it are related by a single flat homography that cannot model either device's lens distortion or its mounting error. A rectangle carried through that homography into the other space looks fine and can be wrong in a way nothing catches.

So a bin boundary is a **grid** now: four horizontal line positions and eight vertical ones, matching two rows of four. Drag one line and the entire row or column it belongs to moves with it. And there are two grids, in two files, each set by whoever is looking at the space it describes:

- `state/bin_grid_camera.json` is dragged onto the rectified preview, which is the camera frame warped through the homography. That is pixel for pixel the same picture the classifier crops its training images from, so what gets dragged is what gets photographed. The old version dragged on the raw feed and cropped from the warped one, which is a box positioned against one picture and applied to another.
- `state/bin_grid_projector.json` is nudged with the arrow keys while watching actual light land on actual trays. One pixel a press, ten with Shift held, and every press reaches the projector as you make it. This one is edited with the projector alone, because the only picture of that space is the table itself: the editing and the checking are one act.

Both start from the same seed off the CAD chain. Neither ever reads the other again.

# The state machine, and the three ways a session ends

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-state-machine.svg]
*Eight states, and the one function all three exits share.*

The table's states are BOOT, UNCALIBRATED, IDLE, SELECTING, BROTH, SPICE, CHECKOUT and SETTING. Boot lands on UNCALIBRATED when the homography or a bin grid is missing, which is the first-run path and has to work on a fresh clone with an empty `state/` directory. A table in UNCALIBRATED can be waved at all day and nothing starts, because a table that does not know which tray is which must not weigh food out of one and charge for it.

**Two predicates, and they stopped agreeing the day the checkout screens landed.** `serving` is true in IDLE, SELECTING, BROTH, SPICE and CHECKOUT: the table is open for business. `weighing` is true in IDLE and SELECTING only: the load cells may still move the cart. A diner choosing a broth is very much being served and must not be weighed, because a hand brushing a tray while they read, or the cells drifting a gram while the QR code is up, would change a total they have already been shown. Pressing Back from the broth screen returns to SELECTING, which is in the weighing set, so the cart unfreezes. That is the entire point of that edge: they went back to change what is in the bowl.

**Three edges end a session, and all three call the same function.** Cancel, payment landing, and switching Serving back on. That function re-baselines rather than re-tares: `start_g[i] = live_g[i]`, so nothing becomes zero and the next diner's subtraction starts from whatever is physically in the bin right now.

Setting mode's exit does one thing first, and leaving it out mis-bills silently. Weighing is switched off for the whole of setting mode, which is the point of the mode: staff lifting a tray out is not a diner's pick. So by the time the mode is exited, every bin's live weight is still whatever it read when the mode was entered. Re-baselining at that moment copies the **old** weight into the baseline, and the next tick reads the new one. Swap a full tray for an empty one during setting mode, which is exactly what the mode is for, and the difference is the whole tray, and the next diner pays for it. So the exit reads all eight cells again before it re-baselines, and that ordering lives inside the state machine where no caller can perform two of the three steps and forget the third.

**The ninety-second checkout timeout is gone.** The reasoning for it was about a table left standing after a diner walks away. What it actually did was fire on a diner who had just got their phone out and was lining up the camera on the projected code, which takes longer than most interactions and is the only thing anybody is doing on that screen. Its entire population was people using it correctly, and it deleted the code mid-scan with the order already written and unpaid. What replaced it is two buttons and the payment itself: a table left genuinely abandoned now sits on the QR screen until staff touch it, which is visible and recoverable, where the old behaviour was invisible and cost the diner their scan.

# Price is a subtraction, never an accumulation

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-cart-weights.svg]
*One bin, one session, three arrays of eight floats.*

The cart holds exactly three arrays of eight numbers: the weight each bin held when the session began, the weight it holds right now, and the weight the table is currently showing. Every price in the system is derived from two of those, on every tick, from scratch:

```
removed_g = start_g - live_g
price     = removed_g / 100 x the rate per 100 g
```

Nothing anywhere adds up individual pick events. That is what makes tipping food back work: it is the same subtraction with a smaller answer, and it needs no code of its own. It is also what makes the running total honest during a rearrangement, because there is no accumulated history to get out of step with the trays.

The third array is the deadband. The shown weight snaps to the live weight only once the two differ by more than ten grams, so a bin's price holds still while the cell wobbles. That floor is not arbitrary: four of the eight channels on this rig sit at 750 to 1500 counts of noise, and the predicate that decides whether an order is in progress reads the deadbanded number. Drop the deadband under the noise and every bin reads as permanently active, and setting mode becomes unreachable, because you can only enter it with an empty cart.

**A bin the scale cannot weigh reports nothing, and reporting nothing is load bearing.** An uncalibrated cell, a missing board and a stale link all answer `None`, which contributes to no price at all. The obvious alternative, 0.0 grams, would read as a bin somebody just emptied into their bowl, and would bill for it.

There is one more entry point, for one moment. Every bin starts life holding a placeholder weight so that the developer panel's mock pick and put-back controls have something to remove grams from before a cell has ever reported. The first time a real reading arrives for a bin, the gap between that fiction and the real number would otherwise price as one enormous phantom scoop. So the first real reading per bin **seeds** rather than sets: it moves the whole session onto the real weight without pricing the difference. The same call is what the setting-mode exit uses for all eight at once.

# Ten and a half readings a second

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-scale-filter.svg]
*From the serial line to the cart, and every timing that follows from the real rate.*

The architecture document said the load cells would arrive at about 78 Hz. Measured on the rig, they arrive at 10.7. The HX711 sits at its default 10 samples per second with the rate pin low, and once that number was actually measured every derived timing in the file changed with it. One sample is 93 milliseconds. A median of five spans 465 milliseconds of wall clock and takes about 280 to cross to a new value after a pick. A 300 millisecond settle window is about four samples, rather than the twenty-four it would be at the documented rate.

That is why the window sizes are constructor parameters instead of constants. If the 280 millisecond lag ever shows on the projected surface, the first move is a median of three, in software, rather than a board modification.

There are two filter stages and they do different jobs. The **median** discards a single bad HX711 read, which a mean would smear across the whole window instead. The **moving average** sits on top of the median's output and smooths the residual sample-to-sample wobble, which is not an outlier at all and which a median therefore cannot touch. Both windows are adjustable live from the dashboard's Developer tab, while a plot of raw against filtered redraws next to the sliders, so "is this filter helping" is a question a person can answer by looking rather than by reasoning.

**One slot, no queue.** The reader thread writes the newest sample into a single slot under a lock and the 60 Hz main loop reads it. A queue would let the main loop fall behind and then bill from weights that are seconds old. The main loop reading the same sample nine times in a row is correct behaviour at 10.7 Hz, and looks like a bug only if you are expecting 78.

**Settled means anchored, not adjacent.** A bin counts as settled once its gram value has stayed within two grams of the value the window opened at, for 300 milliseconds. Compared against the previous sample instead, a slow pour of one gram per sample sits inside a two gram step forever and the bin reads settled while food is still going in.

**The thread never dies.** Unplugging the board raises from inside the blocking read, and a dead reader thread would be worse than a dead device: staleness is what makes the fault visible on the dashboard, and it only keeps working as long as something is still trying. So the loop catches broadly and reopens the port on the same one-to-ten-second ladder every reconnecting client in the system uses. A missing port at startup is the ordinary first case rather than an error.

# The table lights the food it is describing

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-light-pass.svg]
*Five layers into one framebuffer, and the one that goes last.*

This is the constraint the whole visual design is built around: **the projected field is the room's only light source and it is also the entire user interface.** There is one lamp in this system and it is drawing the prices.

So a bin's interior is flat, pure, opaque white. Always, in every mode, all eight of them. That white is what lights the lotus root a diner is looking at, and it is also the background of the photographs the classifier takes of its own trays for training. Anything patterned inside a cutout would be lighting the food with the user interface, and would put the interface into the training set.

The frame is five layers into one framebuffer: the warm near-white table background, the fluid, the halos, the interface, and the light pass, then one keystone warp onto the window. The layers are numbered bottom to top the way the eye reads them, and the light pass is number three.

**It is drawn structurally last.** Not third. Drawing it third would mean relying on every future halo change and every future layout change happening to keep out of a cutout. Drawing it as the final write of the frame makes it true by construction. In practice nothing sees a difference today, because the halo wraps the bin from outside and plate text sits above or below it, so nothing paints into a cutout anyway. Only one of the two orderings survives someone editing that file next year without reading this paragraph.

There used to be a floor lift too: a per-frame blend of the whole composite toward white, to keep the surface bright enough for the camera to find a hand on it. It was removed rather than turned off, because it meant that no colour on the table ever stayed the value it had been assigned. If the table needs to be brighter somewhere, that is a change to a colour constant, and every colour on the surface is now the colour that was authored.

The fire draws under MULTIPLY, so it **darkens** the near-white table rather than adding to it. That was settled with a bench test built into the app itself: half the screen the same coral rectangle under multiply, half under opaque alpha, projected onto the real surface, look at it, then delete neither and leave the switch in place. The addon's own draw call leaves additive blending set behind it, so the bench deliberately enables additive immediately before each half, to prove the mode being tested is actually clearing that state rather than only looking right because nothing had set it.

# The 440 mm centre column

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-centre-column.svg]
*Every band derived from the bin chain, so moving a bin moves them all.*

The gap up the middle of the table is 440 millimetres of solid plywood, which is 554 pixels of projector. It is the one horizontal span on the whole surface that is clear of every bin and every bin label, by construction, and it is where all the reading happens: the brand mark, the mode banner, the ingredient details, the cart, the total and the buttons.

The design document originally put the buttons in the near-right margin, at stage coordinates 1480, 880. That is precisely where the near-right bin draws its own name and price, downward from its ring, and a two-line ingredient name puts ink from about y=890 to y=1010, straight through the middle of the proposed button. The number predated anybody having measured a real label in a real bin.

The same thing happened to the mode banner. It was specified as a full-width strip along the top edge, and drawn that way, until it was seen on the table: the far row draws its labels **upward** into the 177 millimetre far margin, so a 72 pixel strip along the top covered exactly the names staff have to read to confirm which tray is which, during the mode where they are most likely to be confirming it. Being narrower, the banner is now taller and two lines instead. A 440 by 88 millimetre amber block is still unmistakable from three metres, which was the actual goal all along.

Every one of those bands is derived from the bin chain rather than hardcoded, so moving a bin moves the panel with it. The cart's height is eight rows of 32 pixels, measured up from the near row's bottom edge minus a footer budget, and the info box's height is derived from the cart's own top so that the two cannot be edited into overlapping.

**The buttons are the size they are for a physical reason.** 76 pixels tall by about 155 wide, which is roughly 123 by 64 millimetres of plywood, or a credit card, sitting centred in the margin between the near row and the diner's own edge of the table. A hand is not a mouse. A tracked landmark wanders a few pixels a frame even on a perfectly still hand, so a dwell target has to be far larger than a touchscreen's 44 pixel rule, and it has to be somewhere a person can rest a hand without holding it up.

**There is a stack of pages in that column and exactly one is up at a time.** Broth used to draw on top of the cart, because the cart drew on every screen except payment while the option cards were laid out across a band that straddled it. Two changes fixed it together: the option rectangles moved into the cart's own band, and the cart stopped drawing on screens that are not the cart. A banner outranks a page header, which outranks the info box, because a state that changes what the table is *doing* wins over a description of whichever bin a hand happens to be near.

# Hover, dwell, and a second and a half of grace

Core receives stage-space cursors and hit-tests them against stage-space rectangles. Only the pointer hand is hit-tested, and ambient hands are dropped at a single function rather than filtered at each call site, because one function that no ambient hand gets past is a stronger guarantee than remembering to check a role in four places.

A widget accumulates dwell while the pointer stays continuously inside it, fires at 1200 milliseconds, and forgives a 150 millisecond gap so that one jittery frame does not throw away a nearly complete dwell. The fraction goes on the wire and the renderer fills a shape with it, so what a diner sees filling and what actually fires are the same number.

**Bins get a much longer grace, and it came from watching a real pick.** Taking food out of a bin is not one clean enter and leave. It is a hand in, a pinch out, a hand withdrawn to drop it in the bowl, a hand back in for more: several crossings within a couple of seconds. Wired straight to the per-frame hit test, the catch and put-out sound effects played on every one of those crossings, and the ingredient panel blinked on and off with them. Entering a bin still wins instantly, because there is no reason to delay the exciting edge, but leaving one is held for a second and a half before it counts. A re-entry inside that window is invisible on the wire, and only a hand that has genuinely gone clears the bin.

**The idle wander is a pure function of elapsed time.** When the table has been without a real hand for five seconds, core marks the attract state and pushes down the start time and the bin centres. The tracker is the process that emits the phantom, because it is the one place that already knows, per frame, whether a real hand was seen. So "a real hand always wins" falls out of a single if-else with one sender on the socket, rather than two senders racing over UDP. The wandering path takes 3.5 seconds to walk to a bin and pauses 2.5 there, with two small sine wobbles at an irrational period ratio so the drift never lines up with itself into something a person would notice repeating. Because the whole thing is derived from wall-clock start time rather than from local state, a tracker that reconnects mid-cycle picks the path up at the same point rather than restarting it.

# One hand, one window, seven hundred pixels

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-hand-window.svg]
*Scan tiles, one committed window, and the three measurements that shaped both.*

MediaPipe would not reliably see a hand on this table. The first theory was that the hand's share of the frame was too small, and it came with a sixty-trial sweep showing 83% climbing to 100% with cropping. The sweep was real and its numbers were real, and it was measuring a hand composited into the frame rather than a hand on the table. The very next test pasted the same known-good hand into the same rig frame at the same size and position where a real hand was failing, and it detected every single time, while the real hand failed at every crop, rotation, resolution, brightness and contrast setting tried.

What actually worked was denoising a **hand-sized** window. Three things were then measured on the rig against a real hand, and each one shaped the mechanism:

- Denoising a 700 pixel window found a cold hand 20 times out of 20 at two different positions. Denoising the whole table crop the same way scored 20 out of 20 at one position and 0 out of 20 at another, in the same live session. Something that disagrees with itself that hard is a trap for the next person, not a fix.
- Once a hand has been found through a given crop, MediaPipe keeps tracking it through that same crop with the denoise removed entirely. Acquiring is expensive and staying locked on is cheap.
- Hand the same landmarker a differently framed crop and the lock is gone on the very next call, 0 out of 5, even denoised. Its video-mode tracking state is bound to the crop's own framing rather than to "a hand was recently seen in this feed". A window that **shifts**, same size, re-centred to follow the hand tick by tick, survives.

So the tracker holds committed windows that follow their own hand, and spends any spare turn scanning one 700 pixel tile of the table's padded footprint, tiles spaced 470 apart so they overlap by a third.

**And then it pulsed, and the third finding is why.** With two hand slots and one real hand on the table, one slot stays free forever, so the round robin alternated between servicing the hand and scanning for a second one. Every scan tile is a different crop, so every scan turn reset the tracked hand's lock, and the next service turn had to re-acquire from cold. That is what pulsing is. Giving each slot its own landmarker instance helped and did not fix it; declining to claim a free slot for a hand already inside an existing window helped and did not fix it. A live A/B on the rig settled it: one hand, no pulsing; two slots restored, pulsing straight back; two real hands, both pulsing and out of step with each other. `max_hands` is 1. Two-hand tracking is switched off rather than solved, and the round robin, the per-slot instances and the window machinery are all still there for whoever revisits it.

**The cursor is the index fingertip, offset fifteen millimetres toward the far edge of the table.** The original choice was the palm centre, on the reasoning that a fingertip moves wildly while gripping tongs. On a table lit only by its own projection, the palm centre is directly under the hand's own shadow, so the cursor was invisible most of the time: not partly covered, blocked outright. Both the landmark and the offset changed together, and the offset shrank when it moved to the fingertip, because a fingertip is already the most exposed forward point of a reaching hand and needs a nudge rather than a fingertip's worth of clearance.

# The calibration that room light defeated

Everything up to here is what shipped. The rest of this is what it cost.

The original plan for camera-to-projector calibration was automatic. Project a known pattern of dots onto the table, photograph it, detect the dot centroids, pair them with the coordinates they were drawn at, and solve. It is the right idea, it is what the projected field being the only light source is *for*, and it produced three real rig sessions of failure.

It hit a white balance loop that poisoned itself: the camera would converge on a value while looking at a mostly dark field, that value would be recorded, and the next run would reproduce it and be measured against it. It hit the 180-degree mount silently. It hit a threshold sweep that measured worse than the value it replaced. And under all of it sat the thing that could never be argued with, which is that the room has other light in it. The approach needs a dark rig, and this project never had one.

The replacement is four handles on a live camera feed that a person drags onto the table's four real corners, with a 4x magnifier while a handle is held down. The handle roles are fixed physical positions, so the operator reads the table rather than the screen.

The old path was deleted rather than disabled: the dot detector, the solver, their tests, the wire messages, the config block, the overlay plumbing and the dashboard card. **2,668 lines removed, 173 added.** The whole feature was a net negative of about 2,500 lines against a manual tool that fits on one screen and works in a lit room.

Two fossils outlived it. The renderer still carries an inverted-field flag that clears to black and skips the light pass, which existed so that white dots could be photographed against a dark table. And the addon patch, the shader work, and everything else in the visual layer still sit on top of an invariant whose single documented exception is a feature that no longer exists.

# Two shaders, and the one nobody was reading

[IMAGE: docs/img/architecture-shader-bugs.svg]
*One resolution pair, two lookups across it, and both of them unscaled.*

The fire is ofxFlowTools, a fluid solver addon. It needed a patch to compile at all: `std::bind2nd` was removed in C++17, and an unqualified `min` resolves against the wrong candidate under MSVC. Those are two lines and they are the boring half of the patch.

The interesting half is two shaders that read a texture at one resolution while rendering at another.

The fire's density field is 1280 by 720. Its simulation grid is 640 by 360. Exactly half. Some shaders render into the sim grid and sample the density field, and one of them, the advection shader, already had a `densityScale` uniform for exactly that, applied to its own cross-resolution reads. The other two did not.

**The buoyancy shader** computes how hard the flame rises, and its weight term is the density at that point. It read `tex_density` at the raw fragment coordinate. So the weight at a given simulation pixel came from the density at half that pixel's coordinates: a phantom downward force at double the coordinates of wherever the flame actually was, and only the top-left quarter of the density field ever sampled at all. On the table that read as bins behaving differently depending on where they sat, and it survived a long time because it looks like a colour problem, a blend problem, or an injection problem before it looks like an addressing one. Hue was ruled out first, by the honest brute-force route: set all eight fire rings to a single colour and look at the table again. The asymmetry was identical. That is what sent the search into the addon.

**The diffusion shader** is where it gets good. Every shader header in this addon defines its fragment program **twice**, side by side, through two macros: one GLSL 1.20 variant and one GLSL 4.10 variant. Which one compiles is decided at construction by `ofIsGLProgrammableRenderer()`.

This app never asks for a GL version. `glVersionMajor` defaults to 2, so openFrameworks hands it the fixed-function renderer, so `ofIsGLProgrammableRenderer()` is false, so the GLSL 1.20 half is the one that has been running on the projector all along, and the 4.10 half beneath it is dead code on this machine.

In the 4.10 variant, the obstacle lookup is scaled. `vec2 st2 = st * scale;` is right there, correct, upstream, and never executed here. In the 1.20 variant, twenty lines above it in the same file, the same lookup uses the raw coordinate.

The obstacle textures are at simulation resolution while that pass renders at density resolution. Sampling them at the raw coordinate clamps every fragment past 640 by 360 onto the texture's own one-pixel border. That border holds 1.0. 1.0 means solid obstacle. Solid obstacle makes the shader write zero and return. Three quarters of the density field was being erased on every diffusion pass, and the solver runs twenty of those per frame.

The symptom was that diffusion at any strength above zero left the buffer blank rather than blurred. Not faint. Blank. It was read as a grid-size problem for a day, and the fluid was rebuilt at twice the resolution to get around it, which is why the density field is 1280 by 720 in the first place.

The lesson is small and portable. When a library gives you two implementations of the same thing behind a compile-time switch, find out which one you are running before you read either of them. The patch fixes both variants of both shaders, and the notes in `of/patches/README.md` say which hunk is which and why, because the next person to update the addon will have to reapply it.

# A model that scores 99.69% and still gets the bin wrong

The classifier is the one part of this system whose model is left out of the committed configuration, and the reason is worth being straight about.

The mechanism is finished. The dashboard's Capture tab photographs bins in bursts at an interval the operator chooses, so there is time to nudge the tray between shots. Those images upload to Edge Impulse over its REST API from a card in the same tab. Pressing Download builds the C++ library deployment, fetches the zip, unpacks it over the vendored tree and rebuilds the inference binary, all as one click, because leaving those as two steps meant a redeploy once sat downloaded and unused for hours while the app kept classifying with the previous model. The running classifier re-reads the model's input dimensions from the freshly unpacked header on every call, so the next live pass after a redeploy uses the new model while the process keeps running.

The training run behind the current model is real: 2,034 captured images, 150 to 192 per class across thirteen classes, ten training cycles of transfer learning, and a validation accuracy of **99.69%**. The confusion matrix over 323 validation samples has exactly one off-diagonal cell, a single dried mango strip called a rusk.

On the table, four bins came up wrong, and two of them were confidently labelled the same thing.

Nothing about that is mysterious once you look at what the number is measuring. A validation split drawn from one capture session shares that session's tray positions, its light, its camera settings and its arrangement of food. It measures how well the model separates those images. It does not measure a tray refilled by different hands at a different time of day, and the standing target of at least four sessions on different days carries an honest "(unrecorded)" against it in the provenance log. One of the twelve catalogue items, dried small shrimps, has no folder in the capture set at all, so a bin holding them comes back as one of the thirteen classes that do exist, confidently, rather than as unrecognised.

So `classifier.backend` in the committed configuration is `stub`, which answers every request with a fixed placeholder label without looking at a single pixel. Pointing the table at the real model is a local edit to `config/system.json`, which this repository deliberately does not carry. And the live pass runs only while Serving is off, so even with the real model selected it never re-labels a bin during service.

What identifies a bin in front of a diner is a dropdown on the dashboard's Bins tab that a member of staff sets in two seconds.

Two things that fell out of that are worth keeping:

- **A human's answer wins and stays won.** A bin set by hand is marked as manually sourced, and the periodic re-scan skips it rather than overwriting it. Without that, the override would be pointless the moment the accuracy problem it exists for actually occurred: the next pass a second later would put the guess straight back.
- **Persistence had to be argued out twice, in opposite directions.** When four bins came back wrong, the fix was to stop persisting classifier-sourced labels across restarts. The next day's report was that bin labels no longer survived a restart at all: with the classifier off, nothing could ever answer those bins again, so every boot dropped them onto a seed value nobody had chosen, permanently, and two bins ended up reading the same name in the same photograph. Every saved bin is restored now, whatever set it. A stale guess is visible and fixable. A seed value that silently replaces a human's answer on every boot is neither.

**And the re-scan only ever runs while Serving is off.** A re-scan during service is pure risk: nothing good happens when a bin's identity changes underneath a diner who is already holding the tongs, and the load cells, not the camera, are what detect the pick.

# What is still owed

An honest list, because a build report without one is a sales page.

- Two-hand tracking is switched off rather than fixed. The machinery is intact and the failure has not been root-caused past "it pulses at the edges with two slots".
- The classifier needs captures across several sessions on different days, and one catalogue item, dried small shrimps, has no images at all.
- The tracker's smoothing constant, the moving-average width and the 700 pixel window are all single-session numbers. They work on this rig, with these hands, under this light.
- Every colour on the table is unverified against the projector until somebody looks at it. This rig has already turned an authored amber into red and a gold into muddy brown.
- The bin layout exists as numbers in two languages, held in step by a test rather than by a compiler.

None of that stops the table working. All of it is the difference between a thing that works and a thing you would leave running unattended in a restaurant.

# The light is the interface

If there is a single idea worth taking from this build, it is the invariant at the bottom of it: **the light is the interface.** A projector aimed at a table is usually a display. Here it is also the only lamp in the room, which means every design decision in the visual layer is simultaneously a lighting decision, and a bin's interior has to be pure white for the same reason a shop has bright lights over the produce.

Everything else, the five processes, the four transports, the two grids, the three arrays of eight floats, exists to keep that one surface honest sixty times a second.
